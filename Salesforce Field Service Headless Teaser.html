Finish the job. Skip the app.

Field service techs work with their hands — gloves on, tools out, often on top of the equipment or inside it. Closing out a job shouldn’t need a screen. This is one clean work order that a tech can close by voice, by text, through a coordinator in Slack, or through Claude, and it lands the same way every time.

A boom is no place for a form.

Hands are busy

Gloves on, hydraulic fluid to the elbow, sometimes forty feet up. The phone stays in a pocket until the job is done — or the day is.

Close-out happens later

When close-out waits for the truck cab or the hotel room, it’s written from memory. Parts get missed, hours get rounded, follow-ups get forgotten.

The app became the paperwork

Every field added to the mobile screen for the back office is one more tap for the person holding the wrench.

What’s happening

Four channels. One record.

Every close-out writes the same fields in the same shape. Only the last column changes, and that column is the audit trail.

An agent that can’t invent a part.

The channels are the easy part. The contract underneath is what makes it safe to let a text message close a job.

It only uses what’s on the truck

Parts are matched against the tech’s own truck inventory, a short list of what’s physically there, not the full parts catalog. It can’t record a part the truck isn’t carrying.

Unmatched words are kept, not guessed

“The blue thing on the jib” doesn’t become a random part number. It stays on the record in the tech’s own words and comes back as a question. The job still closes.

Every close-out names its channel

Completed Via records whether the job closed by voice, text, Slack or Claude, so the audit trail shows where every close-out came from.

Real records, not a notes field

Parts post as consumed inventory and draw down truck stock on their own. Labor posts to the tech’s time sheet. Everything downstream starts from what was actually used.
