It's a math problem, not a judgment call

Twenty-five technicians against a hundred and forty jobs is more permutations than there are seconds in a year. A dispatcher with a whiteboard isn't solving it — they're finding something that fits.

The good plan dies at 9:15

One emergency, one no-show, one job that runs long, and the morning's plan is fiction. What matters isn't the plan — it's how fast the board reflows around the break.

Everyone optimizes something different

The customer wants the window. The technician wants to be home by five. Finance wants the truck off the freeway. Someone has to hold all three at once, every day, before 7am.

What's happening

Who each decision was for.

Optimization is not one number going up. Every placement trades four interests against each other, and the engine is explicit about which one it served.

What the engine is actually optimizing.

"AI scheduling" is easy to say. These are the four things it weighs, and the reason a dispatcher can defend the result.

The customer's window comes first

Priority, SLA and the promised arrival window are hard constraints, not preferences. Customer First is a scheduling policy you can point at, not a slogan.

Travel is the cost being cut

Every candidate is scored on the drive it adds. Fourteen percent of the day in the truck instead of thirty is the difference between eight jobs and six — with no one working later.

Only qualified people get offered

Skills, certifications and the parts already on the truck filter the candidate list before ranking starts. The engine never proposes someone who can't do the job.

The dispatcher can always ask why

Every recommendation carries its reasoning — shortest travel, no overtime, earliest start. A person can override it, and the board re-optimizes around the override.
